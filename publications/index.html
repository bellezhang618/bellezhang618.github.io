Dissertation

Innovation Without a Problem: Repurposing of Existing Capabilities

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang

This dissertation investigates when, how, and why organizations engage in solution-driven search: a form of search not motivated by a specific problem, but by the potential to repurpose existing capabilities. It follows a solution–problem match through its three moments—formation, conversion, and appropriation—in the setting of pharmaceutical drug repurposing.

Essay 1: Formation

Solutions in Search of Problems: Repurposing Capabilities in Pharmaceutical Innovation

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang

Abstract

When should firms organize innovation search around a problem they want to solve rather than around a capability they already possess? I argue that the answer depends on whether the problem provides reliable criteria for evaluating possible solutions. I model the population of feasible solution–problem pairs as a two-sided matching market and distinguish problem-driven search, in which firms begin with a problem, from solution-driven search, in which firms begin with an established capability and search for problems it might solve. I argue that the difference matters when problems are difficult to define: problem-driven search loses the evaluative criteria supplied by a clear problem, whereas knowledge accumulated around an existing capability can still provide a basis for judging fit. I test this argument in pharmaceutical drug repurposing by reconstructing nearly two million historically feasible firm–drug–disease opportunities, including matches firms could have pursued but did not, and inferring search direction from firms’ prior drug and disease development histories. I found that as prior development failures make a disease harder to define, firms become less likely to initiate problem-driven projects, while solution-driven pursuit remains comparatively resilient. The difference becomes even salient when firms decide whether to advance it into further development, and persists even when only a single candidate is under consideration. The findings identify search direction as a distinct dimension of organizational search and show how an established capability can provide evaluative structure when the problem itself offers unreliable guidance.

Essay 2: Conversion

Who Acts on AI Predictions? Opportunity-Specific Absorptive Capacity in Drug Repurposing

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang

Abstract

If AI can surface the same promising opportunity to multiple firms, why are some firms better able to act on it than others? I study this question in pharmaceutical drug repurposing, where prediction systems can identify plausible new uses for existing drugs but cannot determine which organization will recognize, pursue, and develop them. I construct approximately 1.06 million firm–candidate–snapshot opportunities from a dated biological knowledge graph and pharmaceutical-development records, including candidates that eligible firms never pursue. I argue that acting on AI predictions requires opportunity-specific absorptive capacity: not simply having more knowledge, but having prior knowledge that is relationally connected to the particular problem a prediction identifies. I measure this capacity from the position of each firm’s existing drug portfolio in the biological knowledge graph, controlling for the candidate’s underlying technological feasibility. Firms with stronger opportunity-specific position are roughly 10–15% more likely to convert a surfaced candidate and are also more likely to generate an industry-first application. The findings show that AI may democratize prediction, but related organizational knowledge determines which firms can interpret and act on what AI reveals.

Essay 3: Appropriation

From Graphs to Governance: Value Capture and the Incentive to Run Solution-Driven Search

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang

Abstract

This paper asks what makes solution-driven search worth running in the first place: how much of the value a firm expects to keep. When a match becomes a product, its value is split by bargaining, and each side's leverage comes from its alternatives. Repurposing is the favorable case: because a firm owns its molecule across every disease it might treat, it keeps the value of moving that molecule into a new disease unless it licenses the drug out. So the more a firm expects to keep, the more solution-driven search is worth, and the more it should run. I build a deal-level panel on full-coverage commercializer proxy (in-house vs. licensed launch) with a predetermined, leave-own-out appropriability measure and generic-entry timing from FDA Orange Book loss-of-exclusivity dates. The findings suggest that when there is stronger expected capture, it raises solution-driven search by about 12% per standard deviation within firm, and a more developed, de-risked asset earns the firm a higher share of a realized match's value.

Working Papers

Complementary Capitalists: Venture Capital Syndication Groups in China

Brandy Aven, Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang, and Rui Shen

Under review at Administrative Science Quarterly.

Abstract

What makes a network position a source of strategic advantage rather than merely a structural artifact? While certain positions, such as brokerage, are well known to yield benefits, existing research has largely treated such advantages as circumstantial outcomes of network structure, sidestepping a critical question: can firms deliberately configure the network structures that benefit them? This paper revisits and extends the structural-relational perspective on roles to investigate how structurally distinct roles become strategic through their complementarity, and how VC firms learn through rare successes to reproduce these complementarities across time and context. To examine whether specific configurations of structural roles improve syndicate outcomes and whether VC firms can learn to reproduce such patterns, we investigate VC syndicate formation and success in the venture environment of China. Specifically, we test whether structural role complementarity of brokers and non-brokers in VC syndication groups improves exit performance and can be deliberately replicated by VC firms following successful exits. We triangulate longitudinal data from CVSource and Zero2IPO; together they let us track 93,826 syndicate investments in 20,842 startups by 10,097 indigenous Chinese VC firms between 2000 and 2021. We analyze how VC firms construct and reconstruct syndicates over time, testing whether SRC of VC syndication groups is associated with a greater likelihood of IPO or acquisition, and whether firms attempt to reproduce these role patterns in subsequent deals. The findings suggest that VCs strategically replicate structural role complementarity after successful exits, highlighting the potential mechanism of experiential learning of network advantage. To complement the network analysis and validate our theoretical mechanisms, we conducted 14 semi-structured interviews with Chinese VC partners.

The Problem with Fear of Missing Out (FoMO): Peer Influence on VC Decision-Making

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang, Carrington Motley, and Brandy Aven

Under review at Strategic Management Journal.

Abstract

Why do venture capitalists sometimes rush en masse into high-risk uncertain venture that they do not understand? Existing theories leave unclear how decisions and outcomes unfold when firms see peers' actions but can neither reproduce those actions, inspect their reasoning, nor evaluate them through timely outcomes. Our study address the question of how peer signals shape repeated decisions and outcomes when peer choices cannot be reproduced or evaluated? Using 403,653 U.S. investor–startup–round observations from Crunchbase (2010–2017), we construct a network-based measure of peer reliance: the change from an investor's portfolio-fit choice probability to a probability incorporating activity across its prior co-investment network. Our measure of peer dependence captures the additional weight placed on visible, recent, and successful peer actions after discounting the information their actions provide. Our study develops a theory of peer influence across repeated decisions in the context of venture capital that connects peer-driven entry and follow-on investment to performance, and introduces a network-based measure that distinguishes peer influence from co-movement among market peers. Our findings show that VC firms are more likely to select opportunities in areas in which their peers have recently invested. Conditional on initial investment, investments with greater peer dependence are more likely to receive follow-on funding and are also less likely to exit successfully. These continuation and performance patterns are concentrated in dependence on peers with stronger prior records.
