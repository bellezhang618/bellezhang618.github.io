Dissertation

Innovation Without a Problem: Repurposing of Existing Capabilities

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang

This dissertation investigates when, how, and why organizations engage in solution-driven search: a form of search not motivated by a specific problem, but by the potential to repurpose existing capabilities. It follows a solution–problem match through its three moments—formation, conversion, and appropriation—in the setting of pharmaceutical drug repurposing, where a fixed molecular mechanism (the solution) is matched to a swappable disease (the problem), an AI graph foundation model surfaces candidate matches, and licensing makes the cross-firm market observable.

Essay 1: Formation — Job Market Paper

Solutions in Search of Problems: When Ill-Defined Evaluation Pays

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang

Abstract

When does reusing what a firm already holds create genuine innovation, and when does it merely exploit what is known? I model the population of feasible solution–problem pairs as a two-sided matching market and ask when a solution-driven posture outperforms the problem-driven search that dominates existing theory. The answer turns on evaluation noise: how hard it is to judge a match's value before its outcome is known. From Cortellis development and licensing records (2000–2025; about 47,000 deals) and a target–disease knowledge graph, I construct the counterfactual the question demands: for each drug, the roughly 541,000 disease matches that could have formed but mostly did not, which lets me ask who repurposes and who passes rather than reading conversion off survivors. Using risk-set matching that compares each realized match to five feasible non-matches for the same drug and quarter, I find that matches concentrate where the problem is ill-defined (match rates rise from 17% to 21% across terciles of late-stage clinical attrition), and that the pull of crowding into a well-trafficked problem (fan-in) fades about three times faster than the breadth of spreading one solution across problems (fan-out). A calibrated quality-diversity simulation, estimated by simulated method of moments, reproduces the same crossing in value: solution-driven search creates more value than problem-driven search precisely where evaluation is noisy.

Essay 2: Conversion — Job Market Paper

Positioned to Repurpose: Who Converts an AI-Surfaced Match

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang

Abstract

Once a promising new use for an existing drug has been surfaced, who actually converts it into a novel application? When the criteria for success are not yet defined, the ordinary ways of resolving uncertainty break down: the problem cannot be cleanly specified, traded, contracted over, or judged by an outside analyst. What remains is interpretation through what the firm already knows, and there its relational position matters. I hypothesize that a firm positioned closer to a candidate disease in the underlying biology, its drugs acting on molecular targets only a few steps from that disease, is better placed to recognize what its molecule might newly treat. I test this at the opportunity level across roughly 1.06 million firm × candidate drug–disease × snapshot cells that an AI graph foundation model surfaces independent of any firm's choice. Measuring relational position as PFP (problem-finding position), the firm's personalized-PageRank reachability to the candidate disease from the targets its portfolio already acts on, I find a firm better positioned toward a disease is about 10–15% per standard deviation more likely to pursue the match and turn it into an industry-first use, net of firm, disease, and snapshot fixed effects. A genetic-discovery instrument for a disease's biological tractability supports a causal rather than selection-driven reading (IV ≈ 0.51 vs. OLS ≈ 0.39). It is positional advantage, not molecular quality, that lets a firm act on an opportunity whose value is not yet defined.

Essay 3: Appropriation

Keeping What You Find: Appropriability as Incentive

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang

Abstract

Essays 1 and 2 explain how solution–problem matches form and convert but take for granted that a firm will be paid for them. This paper asks what makes solution-driven search worth running in the first place: how much of the value a firm expects to keep. When a match becomes a product, its value is split by bargaining, and each side's leverage comes from its alternatives. Repurposing is the favorable case: because a firm owns its molecule across every disease it might treat, it keeps the value of moving that molecule into a new disease unless it licenses the drug out. So the more a firm expects to keep, the more solution-driven search is worth, and the more it should run. I build a deal-level capture panel on three substrates: a full-coverage commercializer proxy (in-house vs. licensed launch), a disclosure-gated value layer (Heckman-corrected), and a public–public event study for the true split. Alongside these I use a predetermined, leave-own-out appropriability measure and generic-entry timing from FDA Orange Book loss-of-exclusivity dates. Stronger expected capture raises solution-driven search by about 12% per standard deviation within firm, and a more developed, de-risked asset earns the firm a higher share of a realized match's value (+0.04). At generic entry the original owner does not retreat: a repurposed molecule's new-indication rights stay appropriable to the originator, consistent with the high appropriability that makes redeploying an existing solution worth the search.

Complementary Capitalists: Venture Capital Syndication Groups in China

Brandy Aven, Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang, and Rui Shen

Under review at Administrative Science Quarterly.

Abstract

What makes a network position a source of strategic advantage rather than merely a structural artifact? While certain positions, such as brokerage, are well known to yield benefits, existing research has largely treated such advantages as circumstantial outcomes of network structure, sidestepping a critical question: can firms deliberately configure the network structures that benefit them? This paper revisits and extends the structural-relational perspective on roles to investigate how structurally distinct roles become strategic through their complementarity, and how VC firms learn through rare successes to reproduce these complementarities across time and context. To examine whether specific configurations of structural roles improve syndicate outcomes and whether VC firms can learn to reproduce such patterns, we investigate VC syndicate formation and success in the venture environment of China. Specifically, we test whether structural role complementarity of brokers and non-brokers in VC syndication groups improves exit performance and can be deliberately replicated by VC firms following successful exits. We triangulate longitudinal data from CVSource and Zero2IPO; together they let us track 93,826 syndicate investments in 20,842 startups by 10,097 indigenous Chinese VC firms between 2000 and 2021. We analyze how VC firms construct and reconstruct syndicates over time, testing whether SRC of VC syndication groups is associated with a greater likelihood of IPO or acquisition, and whether firms attempt to reproduce these role patterns in subsequent deals. The findings suggest that VCs strategically replicate structural role complementarity after successful exits, highlighting the potential mechanism of experiential learning of network advantage. To complement the network analysis and validate our theoretical mechanisms, we conducted 14 semi-structured interviews with Chinese VC partners.

The Problem with Fear of Missing Out (FoMO): Peer Influence on VC Decision-Making

Belle (Chaoyi) Zhang, Carrington Motley, and Brandy Aven

Ready to submit to Strategic Management Journal.

Abstract

Why do venture capitalists sometimes rush en masse into high-risk uncertain venture that they do not understand? Existing theories leave unclear how decisions and outcomes unfold when firms see peers' actions but can neither reproduce those actions, inspect their reasoning, nor evaluate them through timely outcomes. Our study address the question of how peer signals shape repeated decisions and outcomes when peer choices cannot be reproduced or evaluated? Using 403,653 U.S. investor–startup–round observations from Crunchbase (2010–2017), we construct a network-based measure of peer reliance: the change from an investor's portfolio-fit choice probability to a probability incorporating activity across its prior co-investment network. Our measure of peer dependence captures the additional weight placed on visible, recent, and successful peer actions after discounting the information their actions provide. Our study develops a theory of peer influence across repeated decisions in the context of venture capital that connects peer-driven entry and follow-on investment to performance, and introduces a network-based measure that distinguishes peer influence from co-movement among market peers. Our findings show that VC firms are more likely to select opportunities in areas in which their peers have recently invested. Conditional on initial investment, investments with greater peer dependence are more likely to receive follow-on funding and are also less likely to exit successfully. These continuation and performance patterns are concentrated in dependence on peers with stronger prior records.
